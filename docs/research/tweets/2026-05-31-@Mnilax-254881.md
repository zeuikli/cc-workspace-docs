## 全文

Opus 4.8 made high the default effort level and added xhigh above it. Going from high to xhigh cost me 4x more and bought two quality points out of a hundred.

Effort doesn't make the model smarter. It buys it more room to think, and you pay for every token of that thinking.

I ran the same task at all four levels, logged tokens, cost, time, and a fixed quality score, then repeated the whole protocol across five tasks.

The quality-per-dollar curve flattens one level before the top. Here's where the money stops buying anything, and the one task class where it still does.

# Why everyone got this wrong

When 4.8 landed on May 28, two things changed at once. high effort became the default, and a new ceiling, xhigh, appeared in the effort menu. There's also ultracode, a Claude Code setting that pins effort to xhigh and lets the model decide on its own when to spin up a background workflow.

The reflex was predictable. People saw xhigh, read it as "best," set it globally, and moved on. The logic sounds airtight: my work matters, so give it the most effort available.

But effort is not a quality dial. It's a thinking budget. A higher level lets the model spend more tokens reasoning before it commits to an answer. On a hard problem that extra room finds things a smaller budget misses. On a routine one the model reaches a good answer early and then spends the rest of the budget re-checking work it already finished. You are billed for those tokens either way.

This matters more than it looks, because of the math of habit. Most of what you hand the model is routine: a fix, a rename, a test, a small feature. If you pin everything to xhigh, you're paying roughly 4x across your entire workload to chase a two-point lift you can't even see on most of it.

For someone running a few hundred tasks a week, that's the difference between a modest monthly bill and one that's quietly four times larger, with nothing in the output to justify it.

So the real question was never "is xhigh better." It's narrower: for which tasks does the extra budget convert into output you can actually measure. I stopped guessing and logged it.

## How I measured it

One task, run clean at each of the four levels: low, medium, high, xhigh. Same prompt, same codebase, same model, fresh context on every run. The only variable was the effort level, set through /effort.

Three rules kept it honest:

**1. Quality had to be objective, not a feeling.**
The primary task was a refactor of a 600-line module that shipped with a 40-test suite. Each run was scored 0 to 100 on a fixed rubric: 40 points for tests passing, 25 for edge cases handled, 25 for no new regressions, 10 for type safety preserved.

A script computed the score from the test runner and a static check. I never graded by eye, because the moment you grade effort by eye you find the quality you expected to find.

**2. Fresh context every run.**
Resuming a session carries cached state that skews both cost and the model's starting point. Each run began cold so the only thing separating them was the effort level.

**3. Median of three.**
Effort runs vary, so each level was run three times and I took the median on every metric. The spread was tight where it counted: quality varied by about a point across the three high runs and under a point across the three xhigh runs, so the flatline between them isn't noise hiding a real gain.

Cost varied more, which only sharpens the point, since the expensive level is also the least predictable to budget for.

For every run I logged four numbers: output tokens, dollar cost, wall-clock time, and the quality score. From those I derived the one that actually decides this question: quality per dollar. Then I ran the same protocol across five different tasks.

# The curve

[圖 1]

Read it from the bottom up. low is fast and cheap and wrong often enough to be useless: 68 out of 100.

medium is a real jump, +17 quality points for roughly double the cost of low, because the model is now doing actual work instead of pattern-matching its way to something plausible.

**high is the sweet spot:** 93 out of 100, under a dollar, around 80 seconds. The tests pass, the regression low missed is caught, the types hold.

Then xhigh. Four times the cost of high. More than three times the wall-clock time. **Two more quality points.**

On a quality-per-dollar basis the line falls off a cliff between high and xhigh: from 95 down to 24. You're paying about four dollars for what high delivers for one, and the two outputs are almost indistinguishable when you diff them.

[圖 2]

## Why it flattens

[圖 3]

From low to high, output tokens climb and quality climbs with them, because the model is genuinely working: reading the module, tracing the test suite, considering edge cases, catching the regression that low walked straight past. Those extra tokens are buying reasoning that shows up in the result.

At xhigh the token count explodes again, roughly 3x over high, and quality barely moves. The reason is simple. The model already solved the task at high. The extra budget gets spent re-verifying a solution that was already correct, exploring branches that loop back to the same answer, and explaining its work at length.

On the primary task, the xhigh run re-derived the same twelve-line fix twice from two angles, confirmed they matched, then wrote a four-hundred-word justification of a change high had made in a sentence. More thinking, almost no new output.

That's the entire insight. Effort scales the thinking budget linearly with cost. Quality scales with that budget right up until the task is solved, and then it goes flat. The gap between those two lines is money you set on fire.

## Where the thesis breaks

[圖 4]

Across the five tasks, moving from high to xhigh bought an average of under three quality points for roughly 3.8x the cost. Four of the five behaved like the primary task. The refactor went 93→95. A single-file bug fix went 96→96, no change at all. A unit-test job went 90→92. A doc-generation task went 88→89. All of them flatlined after high.

> One task didn't. The cross-file migration, the only genuinely hard problem in the set, went 78→87 at xhigh.

high produced a migration that compiled cleanly but broke two call sites it never reasoned about, because tracing every consumer of the changed interface didn't fit inside the high budget.

xhigh had room to walk the full dependency graph and caught both before they shipped.

The detail that sold me: the migration renamed a field on a shared type and updated the obvious consumers. high found the eleven call sites that imported the type directly and stopped there. xhigh kept going and found two more that received the object through a generic wrapper, never naming the type at all — the kind of indirection you only catch by following the data instead of grepping for the name.

Those two were exactly the call sites that would have failed silently in production.

**So the rule, stated without spin: on tasks the model can solve inside the high budget, xhigh is waste. On tasks that genuinely overflow it — multi-file reasoning, large migrations, anything that requires holding a lot of state at once — the extra budget earns its cost.**

The skill is knowing which kind of task you're holding before you set the level. The migration is the tell: if you can't fit the whole problem in your own head at once, the model probably can't fit it in the high budget either.

# Fast mode is a different lever entirely

[圖 5]

Fast doesn't touch quality and it doesn't touch token count. It touches time. Run high in Fast mode and you get the same 93-point output in about a third of the time for double the money. On a quality-per-dollar axis that's strictly worse. On a quality-per-minute axis it wins clearly.

Fast is a speed purchase, not a quality purchase. Reach for it when latency is the bottleneck: an agent a user is sitting and waiting on, a CI step holding up a merge. Don't reach for it expecting better answers. It buys you the same answer sooner.

## Which level for which task

[圖 6]

- **low**: throwaway work — formatting, a commit message, a rename you'll re-read anyway
- **medium**: well-scoped single-file edits where you can verify the result at a glance
- **high** (default): real features, refactors, tests, reviews — leave it there and stop fiddling
- **xhigh** (reserved): large migrations, multi-file reasoning, security passes, anything where a wrong answer is expensive and the task genuinely overflows the high budget

The point is not "use less effort." It's "stop paying xhigh prices for high-class work," which is most of the work you do.

## How to wire this up

This is two settings and one habit.

```bash
# check what you're actually defaulting to
/effort
# pin the sane default
/effort high
```

With high as the floor, every routine task pays high prices.

```bash
# only for the hard task in front of you, then back to high
/effort xhigh
```

Treat the command as a deliberate decision each time. For the migration class specifically, ultracode is the better instrument than a manual xhigh — it pins effort to xhigh and lets the model decide when to fan a task out into a background workflow.

## What didn't move the needle

- **Better prompts at low effort**: narrowed the gap by a handful of points, never closed it. Effort and prompt quality aren't substitutes; they stack.
- **Running xhigh twice and keeping the better result**: the variance between two xhigh runs was smaller than the gap back down to high, so picking the better of two bought almost nothing but spend.
- **Watching the live token stream to stop a run early**: can't reclaim the thinking budget mid-run; cutting it off hands you a half-finished answer. The only real lever is the level you set going in.

## What the habit costs over a month

Say you run 300 model tasks a week and roughly 85% are routine. Pin everything to xhigh and you're paying about $3.90 a task across all 300. Default to high and reserve xhigh for the 15% that genuinely need it, and the routine 255 drop to roughly $0.98 each.

That's the difference between a weekly bill near $1,170 and one near $425. Over a month: roughly **$4,700 vs $1,700**, and the expensive version scored the same on 85% of the work.

# The mental model

Stop treating effort as a quality setting. Treat it as a thinking budget you pre-purchase.

You are not buying a smarter model at xhigh. It's the same model. You're buying it permission to think for longer before it answers. That permission is worth a great deal when the problem is hard enough to use all of it, and worth nothing when the problem was already solved two levels down.

The default landed on high for a reason. For most of what you ask it to do, high is the level where the model finishes the job. Everything above that is the model thinking out loud on your invoice.

# Before you change anything

Pull your last week of xhigh runs and sort them into two piles: the check-it-twice class, and routine work you slammed to max out of habit. If you're anything like me, the second pile is most of them, and most of it would have scored the same at high for a quarter of the cost.

Set high as your default and leave it alone. Reserve xhigh for the task class that genuinely overflows it.

Your output won't get worse. Your bill will.

## 研究摘要

**TL;DR**：Opus 4.8 四個 effort level 量化實測：high→xhigh 花 4 倍費用只換 2 分品質，只有真正 overflow high budget 的跨檔案任務（78→87）才值得用 xhigh。

### 核心論點

- **Effort = 思考預算，不是品質撥桿**：更高 effort 讓模型用更多 tokens 推理；問題一旦解決，多餘 tokens 只是重複驗證，但仍全額收費
- **High 是 sweet spot**：93/100 品質、<$1、80 秒；xhigh 4x 費用 quality-per-dollar 從 95 跌到 24
- **唯一值得 xhigh 的任務類別**：跨檔案 migration/multi-file 推理——high budget 放不下完整依賴圖時，xhigh 的 ROI 才成立（cross-file migration：78→87）
- **Fast mode 是獨立維度**：速度購買，不是品質購買；2x 費用換 2.5x 速度，適合 latency-sensitive 場景
- **月費計算**：300 任務/週，85% 日常全用 xhigh = $4,700/月；智慧路由 = $1,700/月，省 $3,000 輸出品質沒差

### 關鍵數據 / 工具

- 測試結果：low=68 / medium=85 / high=93 / xhigh=95（五個任務中位數）
- Quality-per-dollar：low≈50 / medium≈42 / high≈95 / xhigh≈24
- Cross-file migration（唯一 xhigh ROI 案例）：high=78 → xhigh=87（+9 分）
- 月費差異：全 xhigh $4,700 vs 智慧路由 $1,700（85% 任務同分）
- 評分 rubric：40% 測試通過 / 25% 邊界案例 / 25% 無新回歸 / 10% 類型安全
- 測試方法：三次中位數、cold context、機器評分（非主觀判斷）

### 批判性觀察

- **樣本規模偏小**：5 個任務 × 3 次，只有 1 個任務顯示 xhigh 有 ROI，統計顯著性有限
- **評分 rubric 限於可機器驗證的程式碼任務**：對架構設計、創意寫作等 subjective 輸出的結論不直接適用
- **自動化場景未討論**：CI/CD pipeline 無法手動判斷任務類型，如何實作動態 effort 路由沒有答案
