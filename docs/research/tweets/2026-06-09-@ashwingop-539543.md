## 核心論點

1. 社群媒體 Feed 本質是 Company Brain：記憶人的行為 → 建模 → 路由注意力；公司需要同樣機制但目標不同（協調進展而非最大化參與）
2. 個人 AI 讓公司更快產出 artifacts，但協調成本同步上升，造成「local motion」快於「shared state」
3. Search 是弱版 Company Brain（找你已知道需要的）；Company Brain 告訴你「你不知道你需要什麼」
4. Permission 必須在記憶層本身：來源 / 摘要 / 衍生含義 三層各自需要存取控制（否則記憶變監控）
5. 架構原則：Agent 行動前問 what matters → 行動中查 constraints → 行動後記錄 what changed → 被修正後更新 memory

## 全文

Company brain already exists, just not in the place enterprise software people keep looking for it. You use it every day. It is called X, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, YouTube, Facebook. That sounds like a category error because these are consumer products, not internal tools, and nobody opens Instagram thinking they are interacting with an organizational intelligence system. But that is basically what the feed is. It is an AI system representing the platform’s interests to the outside world. It watches a multiplayer network, remembers the small signals people leave behind, models who creates, who reacts, who returns, which formats spread, which relationships pull people back, and then does the one thing that matters most: it decides what each person sees next.

The feed does not need to command anyone. It routes attention, and routing attention for long enough shapes the behavior of the whole network. That is what makes it uncomfortable. The feed is not admirable. It is instructive. Social platforms learned how to convert memory, prediction, incentives, and attention routing into a system that moves billions of people without ever issuing an order. The objective is usually engagement, retention, distribution, creator incentives, ad performance, and network growth, and you can dislike that objective while still recognizing the architecture. It is a memory system with a utility function, operating across a multiplayer environment.

That is the best existing analogy for company brain. A company should not copy the moral logic of the consumer feed, obviously, but the company is also a multiplayer system. It has goals, dependencies, politics, promises, stale assumptions, customers, roadmaps, incentives, and constant coordination costs. The weird thing is that social platforms have built external-facing company brains with extraordinary sophistication, while most actual companies still run their internal attention system through Slack recency, email panic, calendar politics, dashboards, managers' memory, and whatever got escalated loudly enough this week.

A customer asks for SOC 2 export controls. Sales says yes because the deal matters. Product thinks it is a Q3 maybe. Engineering thinks the related audit-log work was cut last month. Legal thinks the renewal language was never approved. Support has heard the same complaint from three accounts, but under three different names. The CEO tells the board that the enterprise motion is accelerating. Everyone is acting rationally from the context they have, and the company is still confused. That is the problem AI has barely touched.

## Personal AI makes the company louder

Most AI tools today are built around the individual. They help me write faster, code faster, summarize faster, search faster, respond faster. That is useful, and in some cases already table stakes. But a company is not a pile of productive individuals. A company is a multiplayer system where the hard part is making sure the consequence of one person’s work reaches the people and systems that now need to change.

Personal AI can quietly make companies more fragmented. If every employee has a private assistant, the company gets more docs, more tickets, more code, more plans, more internal chatter, more polished updates, more plausible analysis. The output goes up. The coordination burden goes up with it. The senior team sees more artifacts and thinks the organization is moving faster, but often what has actually happened is that the company has become better at producing local motion faster than it has become better at producing shared state.

A company does not usually fail because nobody can draft a memo. It fails because the right context does not reach the right person at the right time, or because it reaches them after the decision has already become expensive to reverse. Sales promises something product cannot support. Product changes packaging without sales understanding the implication. Engineering makes a tradeoff that support discovers three weeks later in a customer escalation. A founder makes a decision in one meeting and half the company keeps operating under the old assumption because the decision never became memory in the places where work actually happens.

The missing AI is not another assistant for one employee. The missing AI is a system that represents the company itself. Not the CEO’s version of the company, not HR’s, not engineering’s, not sales’, not the version in the board deck, not the version in the all-hands. The company as it is actually behaving through its decisions, commitments, exceptions, disagreements, and work. That sounds grand until you remember that the feed already does this every second for consumer platforms. It does not understand the whole network perfectly. It understands enough to route attention better than the network would route it by accident.

That is the primitive companies need, with a different target and much higher stakes. The social feed routes attention toward engagement. The internal feed should route attention toward coordinated progress.

## Search is the weak version

The phrase company brain is useful and dangerous for the same reason. The weak version is enterprise search with better branding. The strong version is a system that helps the company decide what deserves attention next.

Search is valuable when someone already knows they need something. If an employee asks, “What did we decide about enterprise pricing?” a search system can retrieve a doc, a Slack thread, a meeting transcript, maybe even a decent synthesis. That is helpful. It is also only the beginning of the problem. The real question starts after retrieval: what does this information change?

The pricing decision might affect three open sales conversations, a renewal, the public website, onboarding, the finance model, and the roadmap. It may have been made before a new competitor launched. The VP of Sales may still be using the old assumption. Engineering may have deprioritized a feature based on the old packaging model. The board deck may contain a claim that is now stale. A searchable archive can find the artifact. A company brain has to understand the consequence.

Memory is central for the same reason. A feed without memory is a random stream. It remembers reaction history, novelty, timing, and decay because those things shape what should appear next. A company brain needs a different kind of memory: decisions, promises, owners, exceptions, evidence, permissions, and the assumptions people keep forgetting changed. The consumer feed remembers for engagement. A company brain remembers for work.

The distinction matters because companies already have more information than they can use. Meetings are transcribed. Slack is searchable. Customer calls are recorded. Tickets are tagged. Code changes are logged. CRM fields are updated, at least in theory. The company still forgets because memory is not retrieval, retrieval is not relevance, and relevance is not action. A real company brain has to answer a harder question: given what the company is trying to do, what should each person or agent know now that they would otherwise miss?

For a salesperson, that may mean a customer is blocked because product made a change yesterday. For an engineer, it may mean a “small” bug affects a customer promised in the current quarter. For a founder, it may mean three teams are making decisions based on incompatible assumptions. For a manager, it may mean someone is waiting on a decision nobody realizes they own. For product, it may mean the same complaint has appeared in support, sales, onboarding, and churn notes under different names, which means it is no longer a complaint. It is a pattern.

That is closer to a feed than a database. Search is what you use when you know you need something. The company brain is what tells you what you need to know.

## The surveillance concern is real

There is an obvious objection, and it is not a fake one. If you build this badly, it becomes surveillance with better language.

A company brain that asks “is this employee working hard?” is building the wrong product. The better question is whether the company is coordinated. What changed? Who depends on it? What promise did we make? Which assumption is stale? Where is work being duplicated? Which customer risk is invisible because it is split across five systems? Who needs context they do not currently have?

The goal is to maintain organizational state, not to watch people perform work. A good version should make employees less paranoid, not more. It should reduce status theater. It should make fewer people attend meetings whose only real function is context transfer. It should let someone trust that the important thing will reach them without reading every thread, joining every call, or becoming politically close to the person who happened to hear the decision.

That only works if permissions exist at the level of memory itself. A document has permissions. A Slack thread has permissions. A CRM note has permissions. But a memory derived from many sources can be more sensitive than any single source. Ten harmless customer comments can become a strategic risk. A meeting transcript may be visible to one group while the derived implication affects executives, legal, and engineering. A support thread may look operational until it becomes evidence that the company made a commitment it cannot honor.

Without permissions, memory becomes surveillance. Without provenance, it becomes gossip. Without correction, it becomes rumor that has been promoted to infrastructure. So the system has to govern both evidence and derived state: who can see the source, who can see the summary, who can see the implication, which agent is asking, what workflow it is acting inside, and what action may follow. This is one reason company brain cannot be a casual layer over search results. The memory substrate has to carry access, provenance, correction, retention, and auditability as part of the thing itself.

The app is not the brain, even though an app probably has to exist. People need somewhere to inspect memory, correct state, trace evidence, manage permissions, approve sensitive updates, and understand why an agent surfaced something. But if employees have to open a new destination for the company to remember, the architecture is already fighting the way work happens. The app is the governance surface. The brain has to live inside the work.

That means the company brain has to be available to Slack agents, coding agents, CRM agents, support tools, dashboards, and the custom workflows every company inevitably builds because its real process never quite fits the SaaS it bought. It has to show up in the ticket, the customer follow-up, the pull request, the meeting prep, the board update, and the moment after an agent gets corrected. An API, SDK, or MCP-style memory layer is not some developer detail here. It is the difference between memory as a place people visit and memory as a substrate agents can actually use.

Before an agent acts, it should ask what matters. While acting, it should check constraints and permissions. Afterward, it should record what changed. When corrected, it should update memory. Over time, the system should consolidate, forget, escalate, and wake up the right context later. The app still matters because humans need a place to govern the brain, and invisible infrastructure becomes unaccountable fast. But the company brain only matters when it is present inside the flow of work.

## Culture was the first company brain

Companies already have something like a brain. They call it culture. Googliness, Amazon’s writing culture, Apple’s taste, Stripe’s operating rigor, Netflix’s freedom and responsibility. These are compressed representations of how a company behaves. Culture tells people what matters when nobody is explicitly telling them what to do. It carries stories, standards, taboos, taste, and decision rules.

Culture helps a company become one thing rather than a thousand people doing local work, which is exactly why it is powerful. It is also lossy, unevenly transmitted, political, and impossible for agents to use directly. If you joined through one manager, you got one version of the company. If you were in the room for the founding story, you got another. If you arrived after the first hundred people, you inherited a translation. Everyone talks about culture as if it is a shared object, but in practice it is a living compression that degrades as it travels.

A company brain should not replace culture. That would be both impossible and undesirable. The goal is to operationalize the parts of company memory that need to affect work: commitments, corrections, customer promises, quality standards, constraints, decisions that changed direction, and the exceptions that became precedent. Culture tells people how the company behaves. Company Brain tells people and agents what the company remembers.

This matters because the company should not force everyone to see the same thing. Sales, product, engineering, support, legal, finance, and leadership need different lenses. A customer commitment means one thing to sales, another to product, another to legal, and another to engineering. The shared memory keeps those views compatible without pretending they are identical. That is unity without uniformity, which is basically what good companies have always been trying to achieve through culture, operating cadence, writing, rituals, and management layers. The AI version cannot be a super-agent sitting above everyone. It has to be the layer that lets many people and agents act from a governed, shared, living memory of the work.

## The next feed is internal

The feed was the defining AI interface of the consumer internet because it decided what billions of people saw next. The company brain may become the defining AI interface of work because it decides what the organization should pay attention to next. That sounds abstract, but the product moments are not abstract at all: before a meeting, it tells you what changed since the last conversation; after a decision, it propagates the consequences to the people and systems affected; when a customer promise is made, it connects the promise to owners and deadlines; when leadership changes priorities, it notices which existing work is now stale; when a new person joins, it gives them more than documents, it gives them the living context of why things are the way they are.

Every company will need some internal system that represents its goals, remembers its commitments, understands its dependencies, governs access to memory, and routes attention toward coordinated progress. The question is whether that system remains an accidental mess of Slack, meetings, dashboards, politics, and human memory, or whether it becomes real infrastructure. The next great enterprise AI company will not build another assistant for the individual. It will build the feed for the company: not to maximize engagement, but to make sure the company acts on what it already knows.
