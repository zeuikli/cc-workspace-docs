## It Starts With A Default, Not Architecture

Most of this is a mindset shift. An adoption problem. A habits shift.

Before this, I was opening different AI tools, different subscriptions, custom projects and custom GPTs. It felt productive, and it was, but I was repeating myself. It never felt like a co-founder that actually knew what was going on in my life and business.

Step one is to close all those tabs and default to one harness. Claude Code, Codex, whatever you want. Most of you want Claude Code.

Do everything through it and you start building up context, memory, and preferences. That's the way to do it.

An OS doesn't start with architecture. It starts with a default.

From there I think about it in two layers.

The second brain is the first piece, and without it you can't have an AI OS. The second brain is your knowledge. Does this thing know what's going on in your business, your life, your clients, your YouTube channel, and can you ask it questions about all of it?

Once you have a second brain, you build the OS on top. Skills, automations, actually working out of it as your operating system instead of Apple or Windows.

## The Four Cs

The framework I use to build and maintain my AI OS is the Four Cs, taken in order.

1️⃣ Context → who you are, who your business is

2️⃣ Connections → can your second brain reach live data, not just static notes

3️⃣ Capabilities → the skills, agents, automations, and pipelines you build

4️⃣ Cadence → turning those capabilities into things that run on their own while you sleep

The first two are your second brain. The last two are your AI OS.

This is the order I teach. It matters even if you're already set up, because you might need to upskill your team later, or go sell AI OS setups to a business. This is the framework.

## Context: Your Routing Tree

Context is your routing tree, and my Claude.md is the router.

It has some goal and process stuff, but mostly it points the agent to where things live. The rules, the references, the skills, the other projects, the wikis. For my knowledge base I give it the exact wiki path, the hot cache, the master index, and the way to look through it.

People ask me constantly: at what point is it too much context, and how do you know when to split things up?

So far mine has not been too much, because when I ask it to find something it finds it fast. I'm watching it. If it searches for five minutes for a file I know the location of, that's a signal to update the architecture.

We've heard context engineering, prompt engineering, harness engineering. I think architecture engineering is the next art, and there's no single right answer. My pulse check is simple. Is it intuitive to me? Could I manually drill through my folders and find what I need, and can my agent do the same?

One thing I switched to recently that I get a lot of questions about is a folder I call Other Worlds. It holds my other Claude Code projects, the stuff I used to open in completely separate repos.

I moved everything I use frequently into my Herk-2 project for two reasons.

→ Syncing. I push Herk-2 to GitHub, pull it on my laptop, and everything comes with it. I don't have to push six different projects before I switch machines.

→ Reach. My main OS now has context into my book project, my website, my video edits, my token dashboard. It can CD around and find what it needs. I love that I just open Herk-2 and get to anything.

These aren't small projects either. My YouTube OS alone has projects, references, and transcripts with deep drill-downs.

And even with all of that, when I run /context in my main directory I'm only at about 40,000 tokens starting off. Most of that is system tools and a bit of memory. Markdown files won't be the bottleneck for a long time.

There's no right or wrong answer here. You'll know something's off if you're burning tokens or your agent searches for 20 minutes. Otherwise you get to orchestrate your own architecture. As long as the paths are intuitive and the agent isn't wasting tokens, you're fine.

## Connections: Static vs Live Data

Some of your data is static. Your background, meeting transcripts, last year's progress and achievements.

Connections are the data that's constantly changing. Your ClickUp messages to the team, your emails, your QuickBooks P&L.

To find yours, start with a first tier. On a weekly basis, what apps do you open? What bookmarks live in Chrome? Where do you go to talk to people, internal or external? Those high-priority things are what you pull in first.

If you're stuck, think revenue, customers, calendar, comms, tasks, meetings, knowledge. Where does each one live?

For me, Skool is revenue and customers. Customers also on YouTube. Revenue in Stripe and QuickBooks. Calendar in Google Workspace. Comms in Google Workspace, ClickUp, and Slack.

Most of those connections happen through API keys and endpoints, which I'll get to.

The gut check for this whole layer: if you opened Claude Code right now and asked it about you and your business, would the answer sound like a stranger, or like a teammate?

## Capabilities: Skills, Agents, Assembly Lines

Once you have context and connections, capabilities are what you can actually do.

This is another adoption test. Can you default to doing the work inside your AI OS instead of opening a Chrome tab? Before you send that email or pull that report, open your VS Code instead.

When you bring in a tool, check if it has an API endpoint. If yes, grab it. A CLI? Grab that. I lean on CLIs and APIs over MCP servers because I get more control and they tend to be cheaper, but MCP works too.

Skills don't have to be some 10-step monster. A skill can just be a prompt. If you write the same thing every Monday morning or Friday evening, turn it into a skill. If you brainstorm something with Claude Code and like the result, tell it to turn that into a skill so next week it's faster.

Your skill won't be perfect on the first try, and that's the point. Every time you use it, that's data. Tell it what you liked, what you didn't, update it, run it again. I have 20-plus skills plus a bunch of global ones, and every single time I use one I give feedback and say update the skill. A generate-image skill I built four months ago is still changing because my preferences, the models, and the endpoints all change. There's no finished product.

I think in assembly lines, going back to my n8n days. One AI doing one thing really well. One makes the wheels, one the axles, one the hood, and it comes together at the end.

If you cram everything into one session it blurs and you hit context rot. Instead I work in phases. Spin up an agent to research X, take the output, /clear, bring it into the draft, then take the draft into a polishing pass. Chain the outputs through specialized agents.

For parallel work, delegate. Especially with Fable's price, push parallel tasks to cheaper workers like Sonnet or Haiku and get one clean summary back. That's exactly what dynamic workflows do.

## Cadence: Earning The Right To Automate

Cadence is things running while you sleep, and it's the step you have to earn.

You earn it by proving your skills are battle tested and finding the right use cases. Because as you add more AI, the cost goes up, the risk goes up, and the maintenance goes up. Automating something doesn't mean you forget about it. You still need visibility, you still check in, and someone still owns it.

From there it comes down to the trigger.

→ Manual: you ask for it, so maybe it stays in your AI OS

→ Event: a new email, a new customer books a call, and a process kicks off

→ Schedule: every Monday, or every Sunday night

And the ways to deploy are wide open. Claude Code routines, loops, deterministic scripts on something like Modal, even having your Claude OS build an n8n automation that you push into n8n. Pick what fits your use case.

Here's the part you cannot skip. A prompt is never a permission layer.

Assume that if it can, it will. If it could send an email, it might. If it could read that database, it will.

We learned this the hard way. An agent proactively picked up a task from a list, interpreted it wrong, decided it needed to write a discount code, and sent it to 150,000 to 200,000 people on our list. A code that was never supposed to go out. We had to issue an apology.

I wasn't mad at the person who built it. We turned it into a case study so the whole team understood the risk and how to work safely. We almost needed that to learn.

The fix is keys, not prompts. If an agent doesn't have the key to the room where it can send an email, it physically cannot send one. That's a permission layer you can actually trust. Then you fix the instruction so it never happens again, and you keep looping. Every slip is data, not failure.

## My Usage Tips

Three things I do to get more out of it.

1️⃣ Treat it as a thought partner, with a grain of salt. Don't ask how to do something, get an answer, and say go. Brainstorm with it, make it play devil's advocate while you do too, and spin up sub-agents for different perspectives. I love having sub-agents or agent teams debate each other so I can make a more informed call based on my gut. These models still lean sycophantic and want to please you, so keep that in mind.

2️⃣ Have it interview you. I use a skill called Grill Me, originally from Matt Pocock, that I tweaked to write brainstorm docs. It grills me with 15, 25, 30 questions and pulls knowledge out of my head into the OS. You can literally tell a fresh setup to use Grill Me to learn everything about your business. I ran one today to plan this entire video.

3️⃣ Verify its own work. This is the most important one. On that relationship-map build, I told it this was a demo for YouTube so it didn't need to be production ready, just easy to understand, and then to use a dynamic workflow to verify everything was accurate and worked. Have it visually check the result and test that a beginner, a software engineer, and a business owner could all click through and get it. Open a Playwright browser and click around. However you'd verify an intern's work, give Claude Code that same ability. That's the difference between a 70% first pass and a 92% one.

## It's Just Folders And Files

The model and the harness are just the engine. The system is what you're building.

At the end of the day, all of this is folders and files. So who cares if you switch to Codex tomorrow, or back to Sonnet 4.5? Every coding agent can use it.

That's why I keep a .claude, a .codex, and a .agents, plus a claude.md and an agents.md. I want this thing as tool agnostic as possible. I run it daily with Codex too, just to see what's better.

You're not learning Claude Code or learning Codex. You're learning everything at once, because you're building your own repeatable IP. Folders and markdown files, skills and routing logic, logs and wikis. Not a Claude Code AI OS, your own personal operating system.

That should take the pressure off chasing every new drop. One day you might run your own harness on an open source model without being technical at all, and it'll just happen.

## Lightning Round

A few questions I get all the time.

→ What does it cost to run all day? Depends. I'm doing knowledge work, not heavy coding, on the $200/month plan. I rarely hit my five-hour limit, and only sometimes my weekly one.

→ Where does my data go? With Claude models, to Anthropic. It's closed source, so if you're handling a lot of sensitive data you may not want a closed-source model.

→ Do I need to code? No.

→ Day one, empty folder, first move? Use the AIS OS GitHub repo I'm dropping in my free Skool community with the course. Follow it and you're running in a day.

→ When it confidently gets something wrong? Check the work, then tell it to update its Claude.md and skills so it never happens again. Always make it show you the exact source for its data.

→ How do live connections work? APIs or CLIs. Search the tool's API docs, hand that to Claude Code or have it do the research, and tell it the exact endpoints. Use scoped API keys so a key can only read your meeting transcripts, not edit or delete anything. Keys, not prompts.

→ What if I ignore it for a few weeks? Nothing too bad. You just sync your data back in.

→ Does everyone on the team build their own? Yes, but learn it first so you can teach them and hand off team-wide skills. Decide where shared knowledge lives, ClickUp or Slack or Notion or Drive, and give everyone read-only access. The worst case is everyone duplicating knowledge and work, and the real blocker is always adoption.
